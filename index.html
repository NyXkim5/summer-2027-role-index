Build something that gets you the interview project briefs by track

Most student portfolios fail the same way. They are finished, polished, and impossible to ask a follow-up question about. Every brief below is chosen because it gives an interviewer somewhere to dig, and because it produces an artifact you can link in a cold email. The pattern that runs through all of them is the same. Ship the thing, then publish where it broke. Being able to say what went wrong and why is the signal that separates a candidate from a coursework submission.

TrackBuild thisWhy it lands
SWERebuild one primitive from a tool you use, badly, then write up why it is hardA rate limiter, a diff algorithm, a job queue with retries. Interviewers can probe it for 30 minutes. A CRUD app dies in 30 seconds because there is nothing to ask about.
SWEFix a real bug in a dependency you actually useOne merged PR in a repo the interviewer has heard of beats five solo projects. It proves you can read code you did not write, which is the entire first year of the job.
AI / MLBuild an eval, not a modelPick a task, write 100 hand-labelled cases, then score three models against it and publish where each fails. Everyone fine-tunes. Almost nobody can tell you if the output got better, which is the actual bottleneck on every AI team hiring right now.
AI / MLShip a retrieval system over a corpus you know coldYour school's course catalog, a rules manual, a sport's stat archive. Then show the failure cases where retrieval picks the wrong passage. Being able to name why it failed is the interview.
ProductWrite the teardown nobody asked forTake one flow in a product the company ships, instrument what you can observe, propose one change, and state the metric that would prove you wrong. PM interviews are this exercise. Arrive with it already done.
ProductShip a tool for a community you belong to and report retentionFifty real users you did not pay for beats a polished mock. Bring the retention curve and be ready to explain the drop-off.
Business / StrategyModel a company's unit economics from public filings and be wrong in publicPick a public company, build the model, publish it, then update it after earnings and show what you got wrong. Consulting and strategy interviews are exactly this loop and almost no student arrives with evidence of it.
Business / OpsAutomate a real operational process for a real organisationA campus club's inventory, a lab's scheduling, a small business's invoicing. Report hours saved per week. Ops interviews want proof you shipped a change that stuck, not a case study you wrote alone.
Data / AnalystAnswer a question that has a decision attached to itNot 'trends in this dataset' but 'which of these two options should they pick and what would change my mind'. Then write the one page memo. The memo is the differentiator, not the notebook.
QuantBacktest something honestly, including the part where it does not workPublish the strategy with transaction costs, slippage, and the out-of-sample period where it lost money. Quant interviewers are looking for someone who does not fool themselves. Showing the loss is the signal.
Mech / AeroDesign, build, and destroy one part, then show the test dataCAD plus a print plus a load test to failure plus the plot of predicted versus actual. The gap between your FEA and reality is the whole conversation. A rendering with no test data proves nothing.
Mech / AeroJoin a competition team and own a subsystem end to endFormula SAE, Baja, rocketry, RoboSub. Own one subsystem through a full design cycle. Primes and space companies recruit off these teams directly and will ask what broke.
Civil / StructuralAnalyse a real structure near you and check the code compliance yourselfPull the public plans for a bridge or building, run the load cases, and compare against what was actually built. Show your hand calcs next to the software output. Civil hiring cares that you can sanity check the model.
Civil / StructuralSit an EIT and say soThe Fundamentals of Engineering exam is the strongest single filter in civil hiring and most students leave it until after graduation. Passing early moves you ahead of your entire class on paper.
Hardware / EETake one board from schematic to bring-up and document the mistakesThe revision history is the portfolio. Show rev A, what was wrong with it, and rev B. Hardware teams hire people who have debugged their own layout errors.
Chemical / EnergyInstrument a process and publish the efficiency you actually measuredA battery test rig, a small reactor loop, a solar setup. Measured numbers against theoretical, plus your error analysis. The error analysis is the part that reads as an engineer.
Defense / GovWork in the open on something dual use, and read the acquisition sideSensor fusion, GPS-denied navigation, logistics optimisation. Then read one real solicitation on SAM.gov end to end. Being able to talk about how the customer actually buys puts you ahead of nearly every applicant.

Getting seen outreach, timing, and being findable

Applying is the part everyone does. These are the parts that move the odds, ordered by how much they actually matter. None of it requires knowing anyone before you start.

MoveHow to do it
Apply inside 72 hoursThe single highest-leverage habit on this page. Many of these reqs fill from the first week of applicants. The green date tags exist so you can sort by what is fresh. A good application on day two beats a perfect one on day twenty.
Find the actual recruiterSearch LinkedIn for the company name plus university recruiter, early careers, or campus recruiting. That is a public search and it is who owns the req. Skip the hiring manager for intern roles. They are not the decision maker at this stage.
The five line emailSubject line is the exact req title and location. Line one, the role and where you saw it. Line two, the single most relevant thing you have built, with a link. Line three, one specific sentence about their product that proves you looked. Line four, one clear ask. No attachments, no paragraph about your passion for innovation.
Make the project the hook, not the resume"I built an eval harness for retrieval and published where it fails" opens a conversation. "I am a junior seeking opportunities" does not. Lead with the artifact and link it. Recruiters forward links, not PDFs.
Be findable in reverseRecruiters do search. Put the exact language of the reqs you want into your LinkedIn headline and your GitHub bio. Add topics to your repos. Pin the one project that matches the track you are targeting and write a README that opens with the problem, not the tech stack.
Referrals beat cold email, and are easier to get than students thinkOne recruiter reply is worth ten applications, and one referral is worth ten recruiter replies. ColorStack and Rewriting the Code in the events section exist substantially for this. Alumni from your school at the company are the warmest ask you have.
Follow up once, then stopOne follow up after five business days. If there is no reply after that, the answer is no and more email makes it a worse no. Move on and reapply next cycle.
What not to doDo not mass-mail every recruiter at a company. They share notes and it reads exactly as it is. Do not send the same paragraph to twenty firms. Do not ask someone to "have a look at my resume" with no specific role attached, because it puts the work on them and gets ignored.
